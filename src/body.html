No prism

Prism cover test. Alternate the cover between the two eyes and watch for a refixation movement. Point the apex the way the eye deviates: base out for an esodeviation, base in for an exodeviation, base down over the higher eye. Raise the power until the movement stops; if it reverses you have gone too far. Turn the dial for an oblique prism when the deviation is both horizontal and vertical.

The prism you need depends on which eye is fixing: in a palsy the sound eye fixing gives the primary deviation, the palsied eye fixing the larger secondary one.

Reading the screen

You face the patient, so the patient’s right eye is on your left. Throughout the app red is the right eye and green is the left eye, as on a Hess screen.

The fixation target is a small light. Its corneal reflex is drawn in each eye, so you can read a Hirschberg test in any position: the reflex of a deviated eye sits off the centre of the pupil, about 1 mm for every 12°.

Tools

Gaze pad
The dark pad between the two cover buttons moves the fixation light, so your finger never hides the eyes. Drag and the patient follows: pursuit. Tap somewhere else and the patient makes a saccade. Release near one of the nine rings and the light snaps to that position of gaze. Like the face, the pad is mirrored: its left side is the patient’s right. Dragging directly on the face works too.
Cover R, Cover L
Tap to cover an eye, tap again to uncover. Go straight from one eye to the other for an alternate cover test. The occluder is see-through by default so that you can watch the eye behind it.
Head
Tilt the head to either shoulder for the Bielschowsky test, and keep it tilted while you cover. With Doll’s head on, the gaze pad turns the head instead while the patient fixes straight ahead. Habitual returns the patient to the head posture they walked in with.
Distance
Move the target from 6 m to 33 cm, then on toward the nose to find the near point of convergence.
Lift lids
Holds both upper lids up, as you would with your thumb, when ptosis hides the eye.
Prisms
Hold a prism of any power before either eye and turn its base in any direction, then repeat the cover test until nothing moves. A prism over the fixing eye moves the other eye by the same angle, which is the Krimsky test.
Patient’s view
Shows the target as the patient sees it, one image per eye, with the angle between the eyes in prism dioptres. Switch the target to Worth 4 dot and the patient puts on red-green glasses: four lights mean fusion, five mean diplopia, two red or three green mean one eye is suppressed. Try it at 6 m and at 33 cm.
Hess chart
Plots each eye’s field while the other eye fixes. The smaller field belongs to the limited eye; the larger shows overaction of its yoke muscle.

Deviation size

Settings offers two sizes. Clear uses larger angles and denser palsies than average so that they show on a phone. Realistic uses smaller angles and partial palsies, closer to what walks into clinic, and is harder to diagnose. Turn the phone sideways for a closer view of the eyes.

A routine that works

  1. Look before you touch: head posture, lids, pupils, the position of each eye.
  2. Corneal reflexes in primary position.
  3. Cover–uncover each eye, then alternate cover. Repeat at 33 cm.
  4. Versions into the nine positions. Compare the two eyes at each one.
  5. Saccades: tap between right and left, then up and down. Watch speed and whether one eye lags.
  6. Convergence to the near point.
  7. Vertical deviation? Tilt the head to each shoulder and repeat the cover test.
  8. Gaze palsy? Use the doll’s head manoeuvre to see whether the reflex overcomes it.

Scoring in Diagnose

Each case is worth 5 points: 3 for the diagnosis, 1 for the side and 1 for the site of the problem. Opening the Hess chart before you answer costs 1 point. The streak counts consecutive correct diagnoses.

What the model leaves out

This is a teaching simulation, not a clinical tool. Each eye has six muscles with reciprocal innervation, Hering’s law links the two eyes, and mechanical restrictions are elastic limits. That is enough to reproduce primary and secondary deviation, incomitance, the three-step test and the difference between supranuclear, nuclear and peripheral lesions.

It does not model pupillary light reactions, forced duction testing, Bell phenomenon, sensory adaptations other than suppression, or the many atypical and partial presentations you will meet in real patients.
